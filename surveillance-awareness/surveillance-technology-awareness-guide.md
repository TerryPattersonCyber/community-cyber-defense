# Surveillance Technology Awareness Guide

A beginner-friendly guide to understanding technologies that can collect, analyze, and connect information about people, devices, vehicles, and activity.

## Why Surveillance Awareness Matters

Modern technology makes it possible to collect and analyze large amounts of information.

Surveillance technologies can serve legitimate purposes such as security, fraud prevention, access control, traffic management, locating missing people, and investigating crime.

At the same time, these technologies can raise important questions about privacy, cybersecurity, data retention, accuracy, access, and how collected information is used.

The goal of surveillance awareness is not to assume that every technology is harmful.

The goal is to understand:

- What technology is being used
- What information it collects
- How the technology works
- How long information may be stored
- Who may have access
- How accurate the technology is
- How collected information is protected
- Whether information can be shared with other organizations

Understanding these questions allows people to make more informed decisions about technology and privacy.

## 1. Security Cameras

Security cameras are used in many environments, including:

- Homes
- Businesses
- Schools
- Apartment communities
- Parking lots
- Government buildings
- Public spaces
- Transportation systems

Traditional cameras may simply record video.

Modern camera systems can include additional capabilities such as:

- Motion detection
- Remote viewing
- Cloud storage
- Object detection
- License plate recognition
- Facial recognition
- Automated alerts

### Cybersecurity Considerations

Internet-connected cameras are computers connected to a network.

Poorly secured camera systems can create cybersecurity risks.

Important security practices include:

- Changing default passwords
- Using strong, unique passwords
- Enabling multi-factor authentication when available
- Installing security updates
- Restricting unnecessary remote access
- Securing the network connected to the cameras
- Reviewing who has access to recorded footage

## 2. Automated License Plate Readers

Automated License Plate Readers, commonly called ALPRs or LPRs, use cameras and software to capture and process license plate information.

Depending on the system, a record may include:

- License plate number
- Photograph of the vehicle
- Date
- Time
- Location
- Direction of travel

Individual observations may become more revealing when many observations are collected over time.

License plate reader systems may be used for purposes such as:

- Locating stolen vehicles
- Investigations
- Parking management
- Toll collection
- Traffic management
- Property security

### Questions to Understand

When evaluating an ALPR system, useful questions include:

- How long is the information stored?
- Who owns the information?
- Who can search the database?
- Can information be shared with other organizations?
- What cybersecurity controls protect the database?
- Is access to the system logged?
- What happens when a plate is read incorrectly?
- What policies govern how the information can be used?

## 3. Facial Recognition

Facial recognition technology uses software to compare facial images.

Two important concepts are:

### Verification — One-to-One

The system compares one image with another image to determine whether they likely represent the same person.

An example is facial authentication used to unlock a device.

### Identification — One-to-Many

The system compares an image against multiple images in a database to search for a possible match.

These are different applications and can have different risks and accuracy requirements.

### Facial Recognition Is Not Perfect

Facial recognition systems can produce errors.

Two important types are:

**False positive:**  
The system incorrectly determines that two different people are the same person.

**False negative:**  
The system fails to match two images of the same person.

Performance can be influenced by factors including:

- Image quality
- Lighting
- Camera angle
- Algorithm
- Database characteristics
- Application and operating conditions

Because automated systems can make mistakes, the context in which a facial recognition result is used matters.

## 4. Location Tracking

Location information can be generated through several technologies.

Examples include:

- GPS
- Mobile applications
- Cellular networks
- Wi-Fi
- Bluetooth
- Connected vehicles
- Fitness devices
- Smart devices
- Location-sharing applications

Location data can be useful for:

- Navigation
- Emergency services
- Finding devices
- Transportation
- Fitness tracking
- Location-based services

However, location information can also reveal sensitive patterns.

Over time, location history may provide information about:

- Where someone lives
- Where someone works
- Frequently visited locations
- Travel patterns
- Daily routines

Review which applications and services have access to location information and whether that access is necessary.

---

## 5. Mobile Devices as Data Sources

Smartphones contain multiple technologies capable of generating information.

Depending on settings, applications, permissions, and services, phones may process or store information involving:

- Location
- Contacts
- Photos
- Microphone
- Camera
- Bluetooth
- Wi-Fi networks
- Browsing activity
- Application activity

This does not mean every application automatically has access to everything on a phone.

Modern operating systems provide permission controls that determine what applications can access.

Review these permissions periodically.

---

## 6. Smart Devices and the Internet of Things

Internet-connected devices are often called Internet of Things, or IoT, devices.

Examples include:

- Smart doorbells
- Security cameras
- Smart speakers
- Smart televisions
- Fitness trackers
- Smart watches
- Connected vehicles
- Smart thermostats
- Children's connected devices

These devices may collect information necessary to provide their features.

The type and amount of information varies by device.

### Before Using a Smart Device

Consider:

- What information does it collect?
- Does it require an online account?
- Is information stored locally or in the cloud?
- Can data collection be limited?
- Does the manufacturer provide security updates?
- Can multi-factor authentication be enabled?
- Who can access the device?
- What happens to the information if the account is compromised?

---

## 7. Public Wi-Fi and Network Information

Devices communicate with networks in ways that can generate technical information.

Network operators may be able to observe information about devices connecting to their networks.

Depending on the technology and configuration, information may include:

- Device identifiers
- Connection times
- Network activity
- Technical information about the connection

Encryption such as HTTPS protects the contents of many modern web connections, but users should still use caution on networks they do not control.

---

## 8. Online Tracking and Advertising

Surveillance is not limited to physical cameras.

Digital activity can also be tracked.

Websites, applications, advertising networks, and analytics services may collect information about online activity.

This may involve:

- Cookies
- Advertising identifiers
- Device information
- Website activity
- Application activity
- Search activity
- Approximate or precise location
- Interaction with advertisements

Collected information may be used for:

- Analytics
- Advertising
- Personalization
- Fraud detection
- Service improvement

Review privacy and advertising controls provided by devices, browsers, applications, and online accounts.

---

## 9. Data Aggregation

One piece of information may reveal relatively little.

Multiple pieces of information combined together can reveal much more.

This is known as data aggregation.

For example, separate datasets involving:

- Location
- Purchases
- Online activity
- Public records
- Vehicle observations
- Social media

may become more revealing when combined.

Understanding data aggregation is important because privacy risk is not determined only by individual pieces of information.

It can also depend on what information can be connected together.

---

## 10. Data Retention

Data retention refers to how long collected information is stored.

Different organizations and technologies may have very different retention policies.

When evaluating a system, important questions include:

- What information is retained?
- Why is it retained?
- How long is it retained?
- Can users request deletion?
- Is information automatically deleted?
- Who has access while it is stored?
- How is stored information protected?

Longer retention periods can increase the amount of historical information available if a system is compromised or misused.

---

## 11. Data Sharing

Information collected by one organization may sometimes be accessible to or shared with others depending on policies, agreements, contracts, consent, or legal requirements.

Possible recipients may include:

- Service providers
- Business partners
- Advertising companies
- Government agencies
- Law enforcement agencies
- Other organizations

Privacy policies and terms of service may explain some data-sharing practices.

Understanding who can access information is an important part of evaluating privacy risk.

---

## 12. Cybersecurity and Surveillance Systems

Surveillance systems also need cybersecurity.

A database containing camera footage, location information, biometric information, or vehicle observations can itself become a valuable target.

Important protections may include:

- Access controls
- Multi-factor authentication
- Encryption
- Security updates
- Network segmentation
- Logging
- Monitoring
- Data retention policies
- Incident response procedures
- Regular security assessments

A surveillance system that collects sensitive information without adequately protecting it can create additional risk.

# Questions to Ask About Surveillance Technology

When learning about a technology or system, ask:

- [ ] What information does it collect?
- [ ] Why is the information being collected?
- [ ] Is the collection necessary for the stated purpose?
- [ ] How long is the information stored?
- [ ] Where is the information stored?
- [ ] Who can access it?
- [ ] Can it be shared with other organizations?
- [ ] What cybersecurity protections are in place?
- [ ] Is access logged and audited?
- [ ] How accurate is the technology?
- [ ] What happens when the system makes a mistake?
- [ ] Can information be corrected or deleted?
- [ ] Is there a publicly available policy explaining its use?

---

# Awareness, Not Fear

Surveillance awareness should be based on understanding rather than fear.

Technology can provide significant benefits while still creating privacy and cybersecurity considerations.

The important questions are often not simply:

**"Is this technology good or bad?"**

Better questions include:

**"How does it work?"**

**"What information does it collect?"**

**"How is that information protected?"**

**"Who has access to it?"**

**"What safeguards exist against mistakes or misuse?"**

Understanding technology makes it easier to have informed conversations about security, privacy, and responsible use.

# Trusted Resources

### National Institute of Standards and Technology

- [NIST – Face Technology Evaluations](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/face-technology-evaluations-frtefate)

NIST independently evaluates facial recognition technologies, including accuracy, false matches, false non-matches, and demographic variations.

### Federal Trade Commission

- [FTC – Protecting Your Privacy Online](https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/protecting-your-privacy-online)

The FTC provides consumer education concerning privacy, online tracking, applications, connected devices, and protection of personal information.

### Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency

- [CISA – Secure Our World](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world)

CISA provides cybersecurity guidance that can help individuals protect accounts and connected technology.
