# Community Surveillance Audit

A structured framework for documenting, evaluating, and reporting how surveillance technology is used within a community.

The Community Surveillance Audit is designed for:

- Residents
- Community organizations
- Nonprofits
- Journalists
- Researchers
- Students
- Privacy advocates
- Cybersecurity professionals
- Neighborhood organizations
- Public officials

The audit does not begin with the assumption that a surveillance technology is automatically acceptable or unacceptable.

Instead, it provides a repeatable process for determining:

**What exists?**

**Why was it acquired?**

**What information does it collect?**

**Who controls it?**

**Who can access it?**

**Who can receive the information?**

**How long is information retained?**

**How is the system secured?**

**How is use audited?**

**What does it cost?**

**Has it accomplished its stated purpose?**

**What risks or unanswered questions remain?**

**What opportunities exist for public oversight?**

---

# The Community Surveillance Audit Model

The audit follows eight stages:

**IDENTIFY → RESEARCH → DOCUMENT → VERIFY → EVALUATE → ACT → TRACK → PUBLISH**

Each stage produces information that can be used in the next.

# Stage 4 — VERIFY

Every important statement should receive an evidence status.

Use:

### VERIFIED

Supported by primary documentation.

Examples:

- Contract
- Policy
- Government record
- Audit
- Meeting record

### REPORTED

Supported by credible secondary reporting but primary documentation has not yet been located.

### VENDOR CLAIM

Stated by the company providing the technology.

### AGENCY CLAIM

Stated by the government agency.

### ADVOCACY CLAIM

Stated by an advocacy organization.

### UNVERIFIED

Information has been found but has not been adequately confirmed.

### UNKNOWN

No reliable information has been located.

# Red-Flag Register

Document issues requiring additional review.

Examples may include:

- No publicly available policy
- Unknown retention
- Unknown sharing partners
- No documented search justification
- No public audit
- Automatic contract renewal
- No clear deletion provision
- Unclear vendor access
- No documented effectiveness review
- Public claims inconsistent with contract language

A red flag does not automatically prove wrongdoing.

It identifies an issue requiring additional investigation.

---

# Positive Safeguards Register

Also document meaningful protections.

Examples:

- Short retention
- Mandatory MFA
- Individual user accounts
- Case-number requirement
- Independent audits
- Public transparency reports
- Strong sharing restrictions
- Human verification
- Public approval before expansion
- Clear deletion requirements

A credible audit should document safeguards as carefully as concerns.

# Stage 6 — ACT

Research should help residents make informed decisions.

Possible conclusions include:

### No Immediate Action

Existing safeguards appear adequate based on available evidence.

### Request More Information

Important questions remain unanswered.

### Request Policy Changes

Specific safeguards appear insufficient.

### Request Independent Audit

Available information raises questions about compliance or oversight.

### Oppose Expansion

Residents may determine additional deployment should not occur.

### Oppose Renewal

Residents may determine the contract should not continue.

### Request Removal

Residents may advocate for ending the program.

Use the:

[Community Advocacy & Petition Kit](community-advocacy-petition-kit.md)

# Reproducibility

Another researcher should be able to understand how your conclusions were reached.

Whenever possible:

- Link sources.
- Preserve records.
- Record dates.
- Explain methodology.
- Document calculations.
- Separate evidence from interpretation.
- Record unknowns.
- Correct errors publicly.

# Methodology Principle

**Document first. Evaluate second. Advocate from evidence.**

Strong community oversight depends on knowing what is established, what is claimed, what is disputed, and what remains unknown.
