# Nonprofit & Community Organizer Toolkit

A practical implementation toolkit for nonprofits, community organizations, neighborhood groups, student organizations, journalists, educators, and advocacy groups that want to teach communities about surveillance technology or organize around a local surveillance issue.

This toolkit is designed so organizations can use the Community Cyber Defense Project without having to build an entire program from scratch.

It includes:

- Workshop plans
- Facilitator guidance
- Participant activities
- Research assignments
- Community audit workflows
- Organizing plans
- Meeting templates
- Coalition-building tools
- Volunteer roles
- Campaign planning
- Digital-security considerations
- Documentation standards
- Evaluation tools
- Follow-up plans

---

# Choose Your Organization's Role

Before beginning, decide what role your organization intends to play.

Possible roles include:

### EDUCATOR

Provide neutral or evidence-based surveillance education.

### RESEARCHER

Investigate surveillance systems and publish findings.

### WATCHDOG

Monitor government use and policy compliance.

### ADVOCATE

Support specific policy changes.

### CONVENER

Bring residents, officials, experts, and organizations together.

### TECHNICAL PARTNER

Help communities understand cybersecurity, technology, and data systems.

### LEGAL PARTNER

Provide qualified legal analysis or representation.

Organizations may perform more than one role.

Clearly communicate your role to participants.

## Expertise

## Community

Who is already working on this issue?

## Goals

Are we:

- Educating?
- Researching?
- Advocating?
- Building a coalition?
- Responding to a proposed deployment?
- Preparing for contract renewal?

Could participation expose:

- Volunteers
- Staff
- Community members

# Workshop Option A — 30-Minute Introduction

## Objective

Give participants a basic understanding of surveillance technology and community oversight.

## Agenda

### 0–5 Minutes — Introduction

Explain:

- What surveillance technology is
- Why the topic matters
- What the workshop will cover

### 5–15 Minutes — Technology Overview

Discuss examples:

- ALPRs
- Surveillance cameras
- Facial recognition
- Drones
- Real-time crime centers
- Location tracking

### 15–20 Minutes — Data Lifecycle

Explain:

**Collection → Processing → Storage → Search → Sharing → Retention → Deletion**

### 20–25 Minutes — Community Questions

Ask:

- What technology exists here?
- Who operates it?
- Who approved it?
- How long is data stored?
- Who can access it?

### 25–30 Minutes — Next Steps

Provide links to:

- Surveillance Awareness Guide
- Research Toolkit
- Community Surveillance Audit

# Facilitator Principles

## Teach Before Persuading

Participants should understand the technology before being asked to support a policy position.

## Distinguish Evidence From Opinion

Say:

> "The contract provides a 30-day retention period."

separately from:

> "Our organization believes that period is excessive."

## Admit Unknowns

Do not guess.

## Avoid Technical Intimidation

Explain technical concepts using plain language.

## Encourage Questions

Participants should feel comfortable saying:

> "I don't understand this."

## Respect Different Perspectives

Some participants may prioritize public safety.

Others may prioritize privacy or civil liberties.

Good facilitation allows evidence-based discussion.

---

# Plain-Language Rule

Whenever possible, explain a technical term immediately.

Example:

Instead of:

> "The platform uses federated ALPR search capabilities."

Use:

> "The system may allow authorized agencies to search license-plate data collected by other participating agencies."

Technical language can follow afterward.

# Participant Handout — Five Questions to Ask

## KNOW YOUR SURVEILLANCE SYSTEM

Ask:

### 1. What is being collected?

### 2. How long is it stored?

### 3. Who can search it?

### 4. Who can receive it?

### 5. Who checks for misuse?

These five questions provide a starting point for almost any surveillance system.

Example:

# Research Team Workflow

Use this process:

### 1. Define the Question

Example:

> Which outside agencies can search our city's ALPR data?

### 2. Identify Evidence Needed

Example:

- Sharing policy
- Agency list
- Audit logs
- Configuration records

### 3. Locate Existing Information

Search government sources first.

### 4. Request Missing Records

Use public-records procedures.

### 5. Verify Findings

Compare sources.

### 6. Record Evidence

Update the audit.

### 7. Identify Unknowns

Create follow-up research.

# Digital Safety for Organizers

Organizations working on surveillance issues should also consider the security of their own activities.

Develop a security plan appropriate to the organization's actual risk.

Consider:

- Account security
- MFA
- Strong passwords
- Staff access
- Sensitive documents
- Participant information
- Contact lists
- Cloud-storage permissions
- Device security
- Messaging groups
- Data retention

Do not collect sensitive personal information merely because it might be useful later.

---

# Organizer Information-Minimization Principle

Ask:

> Do we actually need this information?

before collecting:

- Home addresses
- Phone numbers
- Personal emails
- Employment information
- Political affiliations
- Sensitive demographic data

If information is unnecessary:

**do not collect it.**

If it is necessary:

- Explain why.
- Limit access.
- Protect it.
- Establish deletion rules.

# Participant Privacy

For public workshops:

Consider whether you actually need:

- Registration
- Full names
- Photos
- Video recordings
- Attendance lists

If photographing or recording participants:

- Provide notice.
- Obtain appropriate consent.
- Offer reasonable alternatives where possible.

Organizations should follow applicable law and internal policy.

# Sensitive Community Considerations

Some communities may reasonably have greater concerns about being identified in connection with surveillance advocacy.

Organizations should consider risks faced by:

- Activists
- Journalists
- Immigrants
- Protest participants
- Domestic violence survivors
- Minors
- People involved in sensitive legal matters
- People participating in politically sensitive activity

Do not assume everyone has the same risk profile.

# Accessibility Checklist

Community resources should be usable by as many people as possible.

Consider:

- Plain language
- Large readable text
- Screen-reader compatibility
- Captioned videos
- Accessible meeting locations
- Language translation
- Mobile-friendly materials
- Printed materials
- Remote participation
- Childcare where possible

Accessibility improves participation.

# Translation Workflow

For multilingual communities:

1. Identify priority languages.
2. Translate core materials.
3. Have a fluent reviewer check meaning.
4. Avoid overly technical language.
5. Update translations when the original changes.
6. Clearly display version dates.

Do not assume automated translation is sufficient for high-stakes legal or policy language.

# Responding to Common Arguments

## "If You Have Nothing to Hide, Why Care?"

Possible response:

Privacy is not only about hiding wrongdoing.

Questions about retention, cybersecurity, access, and accountability matter whenever large databases involving people's activities are created.

---

## "The Technology Helps Solve Crimes."

Possible response:

A technology may provide investigative value while still requiring appropriate safeguards.

The relevant questions include:

- Whether benefits are documented
- Whether collection is proportionate
- Whether misuse is prevented
- Whether information is secure
- Whether continued use receives oversight

---

## "Only Criminals Are Being Tracked."

Possible response:

Many surveillance technologies collect information broadly and later search that information for investigative purposes.

Understanding what is collected and retained is therefore relevant to the wider community.

---

## "The Vendor Says the System Is Secure."

Possible response:

Vendor security information is useful, but organizations should also examine:

- Contract requirements
- Agency controls
- Authentication
- Audit logs
- Incident-response procedures
- Independent assessments

---

# Balanced Advocacy

Credible organizations should acknowledge meaningful safeguards when they exist.

Example:

> "The agency requires MFA and documented search purposes, which are positive safeguards. However, we remain concerned that external sharing is not publicly documented."

Balanced analysis can make recommendations more credible.

# Organizing Escalation Ladder

Organizations do not need to begin with the most confrontational action.

Possible progression:

### Level 1 — Learn

Research the technology.

### Level 2 — Ask

Contact the agency.

### Level 3 — Document

Submit public-records requests.

### Level 4 — Educate

Hold workshops.

### Level 5 — Engage

Meet with officials.

### Level 6 — Participate

Speak at public meetings.

### Level 7 — Organize

Build coalition support.

### Level 8 — Advocate

Launch petitions or policy campaigns.

### Level 9 — Escalate

Where appropriate, engage:

- Journalists
- Legislators
- Civil-liberties organizations
- Qualified legal organizations

All activity should remain lawful.
