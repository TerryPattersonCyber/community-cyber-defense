# Surveillance Awareness

Accessible resources designed to help people understand surveillance technologies, the information they collect, their cybersecurity and privacy implications, and lawful ways communities can participate in decisions about how these technologies are used.

This section combines two goals:

**Education:** Understand the technology.

**Action:** Learn how to participate in decisions about surveillance technology in your community.

---

## Available Resources

### 👁️ [Surveillance Technology Awareness Guide](surveillance-technology-awareness-guide.md)

A beginner-friendly guide explaining common surveillance technologies and the privacy and cybersecurity questions surrounding them.

Topics include:

- Security cameras
- Automated license plate readers (ALPRs)
- Facial recognition
- Location tracking
- Mobile device data
- Smart devices and the Internet of Things (IoT)
- Public Wi-Fi and network information
- Online tracking
- Data aggregation
- Data retention
- Data sharing
- Cybersecurity of surveillance systems

The guide also provides questions community members can ask about surveillance systems, including:

- What information is collected?
- Why is it collected?
- How long is it stored?
- Who can access it?
- Can the information be shared?
- What cybersecurity protections exist?
- Is system access logged and audited?
- How accurate is the technology?
- What happens when the technology makes a mistake?

# Education → Awareness → Action

The goal of this section is not simply to describe surveillance technology.

Community members should be able to understand how a technology works, evaluate its potential benefits and risks, ask informed questions, and participate in decisions about whether and how that technology should be used.

A useful framework is:

### 1. Understand

Learn what the technology does and what information it collects.

### 2. Investigate

Learn who operates the system, who has access to the information, how long the information is retained, and how the system is governed.

### 3. Question

Ask whether appropriate privacy, cybersecurity, transparency, accuracy, and accountability safeguards exist.

### 4. Participate

Use public meetings, public records, petitions, elected representatives, public education, journalism, legislation, and other lawful civic processes to advocate for change when necessary.

# Automated License Plate Reader Advocacy

Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs) have become an important surveillance and privacy issue in communities across the United States.

Organizations including the American Civil Liberties Union and Electronic Frontier Foundation provide educational and advocacy resources for people interested in understanding or challenging ALPR deployments.

Community advocacy may focus on:

- Preventing new deployments
- Requesting public hearings before deployment
- Opposing expansion of existing systems
- Asking governments not to renew vendor contracts
- Requesting removal of existing cameras
- Limiting how long ALPR data is retained
- Restricting access to ALPR databases
- Restricting data sharing with outside organizations
- Requiring documented purposes for searches
- Requiring audit logs
- Requiring transparency reports
- Creating stronger local or state privacy laws

# Lawful Advocacy

This project supports lawful, peaceful civic participation.

Community members who oppose surveillance technology should use legitimate methods such as:

- Public-records requests
- Public meetings
- Petitions
- Contacting elected officials
- Public education
- Journalism
- Legislative advocacy
- Peaceful demonstrations
- Voting and elections
- Legal organizations and courts when appropriate

This project does **not** encourage damaging, disabling, hacking, tampering with, or otherwise interfering with surveillance equipment.

Effective advocacy is stronger when it is informed, documented, lawful, and focused on specific policy changes.

---

# Key Principle

**Awareness should lead to informed participation.**

The question is not simply whether a technology is "good" or "bad."

Communities should understand:

**What does the technology do?**

**What information does it collect?**

**Who controls that information?**

**Who can access it?**

**How long is it retained?**

**How is it protected?**

**What happens when the technology makes a mistake?**

**What options do residents have if they want the system changed or removed?**
